# Module 13 · Solutions

## Warm-up

**E13.01** Use only information that was public on the decision date. Hindsight leaks through (1) the choice of case
itself — you know it is famous, so you look for the flaw; (2) looking up later prices or events "just to check a
fact"; (3) framing — describing a signal as a "red flag" because you know it mattered, when at the time it was one of
many ambiguous facts.

**E13.02** (a) Interest income: I1 Satyam (decisive); G9 Wirecard. (b) Borrowing despite cash: G9 Wirecard; I1 Satyam.
(c) Where the profit comes from: G9 Wirecard (three partners); G4 Enron (mark-to-market), G8 Valeant, I11 Jet (other
income), I14 Vakrangee (low-margin revenue surge). (d) Auditor resignation: I14 Manpasand and Vakrangee. (e) Related-
party acquisition: I1 Satyam (Maytas); G9 Wirecard (the Indian acquisition marked up ~9× within weeks).

**E13.03** Variant perception: G10 Apple (a consumer franchise, not a hardware maker). Implied expectations: G3 Cisco
(≈34% revenue growth for ten years). Value by subtraction: I15 Tata Motors (JLR's equity priced near zero). Liquidity vs
solvency: I4 DHFL (a going concern until its funding closed) vs G6 Lehman. Re-rating: I2 Asian Paints (31× → 97.5×).
Value trap: G7 Nokia (low P/E on peak earnings) — contrast I12 ITC in 2020, which was not one.

**E13.04** Auditors test the accounts against records and third-party confirmations (not trading patterns or
counterparties' existence beyond sampling); rating agencies test debt-service capacity, usually with a lag and relying
on management information (I4, G4); regulators protect market integrity and depositors, not shareholders' returns (G9
BaFin, I5 RBI); index providers screen size and liquidity (which manipulation can inflate, I14); ESG raters score
policies; institutional directors rely on board papers; strategic investors (SoftBank in G9, Etihad in I11) may be
buying control, options or deals rather than paying for value.

**E13.05** Rubric. (a) Right reasons: G1, G2 (1989), G10, I3 (A), I15 (A), I12 (A), I13 (A). (b) Unanticipated reasons:
I3 (B — earnings outran a de-rating), I15 (B — recovery after Covid), G5 (AWS). (c) Business did well, stock did not:
G3, I2 (B), I13 (B), I12 (B). (d) Business or accounts failed: I1, G4, G6, G9, I4, I5, I11, I14. Place I8, I9 and I10
with an argument; they depend on which company and decision point you take.

## Core

**E13.06** Satyam: 257 ÷ 4,080 = **6.3%** at most (other income includes non-interest items), against 8.5–9.5%. At 8.5%
on all cash, expected interest ≈ ₹347 crore — **≈ ₹90 crore a year missing**, every year. Wirecard: 20.6 ÷ 3,270 =
**0.63%**; at 2% on all cash, €65 million expected — **≈ €45 million missing**, and the €20.6 million also covered
customer deposits. Wirecard's result is more damning: its escrow money was supposedly in Asian banks paying 2–6%, so the
gap was a factor of three or more, whereas Satyam's could (barely) be explained by current-account balances. Both failed
the same test for the same reason: fraudsters can invent a balance but not the bank's interest credit.

**E13.07** (a) Asian Paints: price 12.18× (25.5% a year); EPS 3.59×; P/E 3.15×. Re-rating share of the log return =
ln 3.15 ÷ ln 12.18 = **46%**. (b) ITC: price 2.72×; EPS 1.35×; P/E 2.02×. ln 2.02 ÷ ln 2.72 = **70%**. (c) Bajaj
Finance: price 75.6×; P/B 6.85×; ln 6.85 ÷ ln 75.6 = **44%**, with book value per share compounding at ~31% a year for
the rest. Roughly half to two-thirds of each return was re-rating — the part that cannot repeat, and in Asian Paints'
and ITC's case later reversed.

**E13.08** RoE = g + P/B × (r − g). (a) 0.12 + 1.3 × 0.02 = **14.6%** — below the 18–20% the company was targeting; the
market did not believe the plan. (b) 0.08 + 8.9 × 0.04 = **43.6%** in steady state, or a long period of 20%+ RoE with
30% growth. What happened: RoE ran at 19–24% through FY2019 and 17–24% after; AUM grew 45% a year to 2019 and 20–35%
after. The 2010 price was easily beaten; the 2019 price was not beaten on multiple but was carried by earnings.

**E13.09** (a) Indian business EV at 6× = ₹14,214 crore; less ₹10,000 crore debt = ₹4,214 crore of equity; implied JLR
equity = 6,081 − 4,214 = **≈ ₹1,867 crore**, against ₹9,870 crore paid nine months earlier. (b) Implied cigarette EV =
2,03,129 − 1,04,607 = ₹98,522 crore = **6.5× EBITDA** (8.9× after-tax PBIT) for a business growing profit ~7% a year.
Common thread: in both, the market's price for the troubled or unloved piece was close to a liquidation or run-off value;
the healthy parts alone roughly justified the whole price, so the buyer got the controversial asset almost free.

**E13.10** Jet: 1,360 ÷ 12,847 = **10.6%** cash cover — an airline eleven months short of cash. DHFL: funding gap
17,876 − 16,290 = **₹1,586 crore** (met from its liquidity buffer); sell-downs were 11,873 ÷ 16,290 = **73%** of new
money and CP only **3.5%**. DHFL could no longer borrow short-term in the market; it was funding redemptions by selling
assets — the classic sign that a lender's liability franchise has closed, whatever its asset quality.

**E13.11** (a) Wirecard: EV = 0.7 × 40% − 0.3 × 100% = **−2%**; Kelly = (0.7 × 0.4 − 0.3)/0.4 = **−5%** → do not own.
(b) Small cap: EV = 0.8 × 30% − 0.2 × 90% = **+6%**; Kelly with b = 1/3 → **20%**; the max-loss rule (2% ÷ 90%) caps it at
2.2%. (c) Satyam: upside +39.7%, downside −55.3%; at p = 0.42, EV ≈ **−0.2%**, Kelly ≈ **−0.5%** → no edge at the market's
own odds; the tests in the case argued for a higher p. In all three, a small change in the fraud probability flips the
answer — which is why sizing, not conviction, is the right response to Amber evidence.

**E13.12** Tata Motors: fixed costs = 30% × 93.3 = 28.0 per 100 of revenue; contribution margin 34.7%; break-even at
28.0 ÷ 0.347 = 80.7 → a **19.3% revenue decline**. Kingfisher: break-even requires passenger revenue per head up
**42.7%** (₹2,010 crore ÷ ₹4,711 crore). Both businesses have high fixed costs relative to contribution, so a normal
cyclical downturn (−20 to −35% in volume) produces operating losses; the balance sheet must carry the business through
the trough. Tata Motors had a controlling shareholder willing to inject equity; Kingfisher's lenders converted debt
instead, which bought time without changing the unit economics.

**E13.13** Kingfisher: 45.70 ÷ 64.48 − 1 = **−29%**. Jet: 295.45 ÷ 754.74 − 1 = **−61%**. ITC Hotels: 171.85 ÷ 260 − 1 =
**−34%**. The banks' ₹64.48 was a formula price (an average of past prices); Etihad's ₹754.74 bought control rights and a
loyalty-programme deal five years earlier; the ₹260 was a thin pre-open auction dominated by index and arbitrage flows.
None measured the value of the business; each anchored investors who treated it as if it did.

**E13.14** Tata Motors (B): 65.30 ÷ 152.65 − 1 = **−57%**, then 4.9× the purchase price by 2026 — rewarded. ITC from peak: **−48%** — not yet
rewarded (below the 2023 buy price). Asian Paints: 2,116 ÷ 3,590 − 1 = **−41%** — not rewarded by 2026. Jet: **−88%**
to the last trade, then zero — not rewarded. Drawdowns in themselves do not distinguish good from bad decisions; what
distinguished Tata Motors was a balance sheet that survived and a business whose earnings recovered.

**E13.15** Vakrangee: −80% by 30-Apr-2018 when PwC resigned, −94% by 31-May. Selling on the resignation would have saved
only the last 14 points of a 94% fall — most of the damage happened before the auditor left, when price and volume were
the signal. Manpasand: −48% by 31-May-2018 (after Deloitte's resignation on 26 May) and −66% by 29-Jun; selling on the
first trading day after the resignation (at about −20%) would have avoided most of the eventual 99% loss. The rule helps
most when the resignation comes *first*; it is a backstop, not a substitute for earlier signals.

**E13.16** Cisco: ≈34% revenue growth for ten years to justify $80 — **impossible** (a $273 billion revenue company).
Asian Paints at ₹3,079: ≈28% profit growth for a decade — **implausible** for a business growing 9–12%. Vakrangee at ₹505:
≈35% profit growth for ten years from a network of kiosks — **implausible**, and the revenue quality was doubtful. ITC
at ₹165.25: ≈4.6% perpetual dividend growth at r = 11% — **undemanding** for a business growing profit 7–9%. The ranking
matches the outcomes: the first three lost heavily; ITC nearly tripled in three years.

**E13.17** Rubric. Satyam: cash yield below deposit rates (verifiable); borrowing and pledging despite cash
(verifiable); Maytas related-party deal (inferential). Wirecard: interest income on cash (verifiable); borrowing and a
convertible while "cash-rich" (verifiable); profits from unnamed partners (inferential from disclosure gaps). Manpasand:
negative FCF every year (verifiable); ₹900 crore raised in two years (verifiable); 62× earnings (verifiable, but not a
fraud signal). Vakrangee: margin falling as revenue surged (verifiable); 5.7× price rise on heavy volume (verifiable but
inferential as to cause); treasury money in another hot small cap (verifiable). Note how many *verifiable* flags were
available in each case — and how few were acted on.
