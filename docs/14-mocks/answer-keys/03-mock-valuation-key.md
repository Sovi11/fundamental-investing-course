## Part B

**B1 (8).** kₑ = 7.0% + 1.10 × 6.0% = **13.6%** (2); after-tax k_d = 9.0% × 0.75 = **6.75%** (2); WACC = 0.8 × 13.6% + 0.2 ×
6.75% = **12.23%** (2). More debt raises financial risk: beta (and so kₑ) rises and the cost of debt rises, offsetting
the cheaper debt; beyond a point distress costs dominate ([06.2](../../06-valuation/02-cost-of-capital.md)) (2).

**B2 (15).** (a) PV of years 1–5 at 12% = **₹348.2 Cr**; year-6 FCFF = 120.75; TV = 120.75 ÷ 0.07 = **₹1,725.0 Cr**; PV of
TV = 1,725 ÷ 1.12⁵ = **₹978.8 Cr**; EV = **₹1,327.1 Cr**; equity = **₹1,127.1 Cr**; value per share = **₹112.7** (7).
(b) TV share = 978.8 ÷ 1,327.1 = **73.8%** (1).
(c) Sensitivity (₹ per share) (5):

| WACC \ g | 4% | 5% | 6% |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|

(d) ₹120 needs WACC 11% with g of about 4% or more, or WACC 12% with g near 6% (2).

**B3 (8).** (a) Reinvestment rate = g ÷ RONIC = 5% ÷ 15% = **33.3%**; FCF = 100 × (1 − 0.333) = **₹66.7 Cr**; TV = 66.7 ÷
0.06 = **₹1,111.1 Cr** (4). (b) RONIC = WACC: TV = NOPAT ÷ WACC = 100 ÷ 0.11 = **₹909.1 Cr** (growth adds nothing); naive:
100 ÷ 0.06 = **₹1,666.7 Cr** (2). (c) The naive formula assumes growth needs no reinvestment — infinite RONIC — and so
overstates value ([06.3](../../06-valuation/03-dcf-step-by-step.md)) (2).

**B4 (12).** (a) Median P/E = (33.1 + 36.2) ÷ 2 = **34.65×** → 15.08 × 34.65 = **₹522.5**. Median EV/EBITDA = (20.1 + 21.4)
÷ 2 = **20.75×** → EV = 181.9 × 20.75 = ₹3,774 Cr − 140.5 − 18.2 = ₹3,616 Cr ÷ 6.07 = **₹595.7** (6). (b) Reasons for a
discount: lower ROCE (16% vs a 19.5% peer median — only Deccan is lower); much weaker cash conversion (CFO ÷ EBITDA
36%, receivables at 96 days); slower growth than Sabarmati and Konkan, the two peers it most resembles in product mix.
Credit also for governance points from the running example (the promoter pledge, the rising related-party share) (3). (c) The peer multiples assume Kaveri's earnings are as good as the peers'; the DCF values its
actual cash flow, which is poor. Peer multiples are themselves high (29–40× P/E), so relative value can be "cheap"
against an expensive group. Trust the DCF's discipline, use the peers as a check on the market's mood
([06.5](../../06-valuation/05-relative-valuation-and-multiples.md)) (3).

**B5 (12).** (a) D₁ = 40% × 25 = ₹10; 500 = 10 ÷ (0.12 − g) → **g = 10%** (4). (b) g = RoE × retention → RoE = 10% ÷ 0.60 =
**16.7%** (3). (c) Payout = 1 − 8% ÷ 15% = 46.7%; justified forward P/E = 0.467 ÷ (0.12 − 0.08) = **11.7×**, i.e. ₹292 on ₹25
EPS. The ₹500 price (20× forward) needs faster growth or a higher RoE than 15% — it is expensive unless the company can
sustain RoE near 17% with 10% growth (5).

## Part C (25)

1. (6) P/B = (15.1% − 8%) ÷ (13% − 8%) = **1.42×** → 1.42 × ₹154.1 = **≈ ₹219**, 46% below ₹402.
2. (5) Implied RoE = 8% + 2.61 × 5% = **21.0%**.
3. (6) *Leverage:* RoA 3.5% × leverage 6.0× = 21%; leverage from 4.4× to 6× means CRAR falling towards ~20% — possible for a
   well-capitalised NBFC, but it raises risk and may trigger rating scrutiny. *RoA:* at 4.4× leverage, RoE 21% needs RoA of
   4.8%, against a six-year best of 3.7%. Credit cost falling from 1.9% back to its FY24 low of 1.3% adds only ~0.4 pp of
   RoA after tax; the rest would need cost-to-income far below 35.4% — better than anything in Nirmal's history, while
   GNPA is rising (2.5% → 2.9%). Leverage is the more plausible route; both require things to go right in a book that is
   currently deteriorating ([07.1](../../07-special-valuation/01-banks-and-nbfcs.md)).
4. (4) 2.4 = (RoE − 8%) ÷ 5% → **RoE = 20%**.
5. (4) The price embeds a lender earning ~21% on equity indefinitely against today's 15% and a rising GNPA: expensive;
   a watch rather than a buy. Change of mind: two consecutive quarters of falling stage-3 loans with credit cost below
   1.5%, or evidence that leverage can rise to 6× without a rating downgrade.
